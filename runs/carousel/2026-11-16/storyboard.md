# Carousel no. 79, November 16th, 2026: a spoken story, and the instruments that have not read it yet

## The story, and what the fact check did to it

UT Health San Antonio announced a one year proof of concept pilot (c3), paid for by a $200,000 grant from the San Antonio
Medical Foundation (c2). A UT San Antonio project aims to turn language into data that could help clinicians (c1). Alicia
Swan of the South Texas Veterans Health Care System is one of three leads (c4). Rocio Norman, a speech language pathologist,
has worked with veterans for 18 years (c5). Many veterans she treated reported trouble finding words or keeping up in fast or
noisy talk, even when standard tests showed no clear impairment (c16), while their words and grammar generally remain (c17).
Most adult language tests were built for aphasia after a stroke or for severe injury (c18). The study draws on recordings
already made at the San Antonio VA, of veterans describing how they were injured and an important life event (c6), at
several points during treatment (c7). After transcription the team plans to assess four named features (c8). Today that
analysis takes slow transcription and coding, often by several reviewers (c10). The first goal is whether an automated method
can find communication differences at all, and how they relate to four named symptoms (c11). The tool is meant to support
clinicians, not replace them (c9). Federal grants follow only if the pilot finds something meaningful (c12).

The fact check rewrote seven claim texts to hold them to their quotes (planned measurement is future, not under way; c19 and
c20 dropped the source's banned word from their paraphrase). No result, accuracy, sample size or veteran count exists, so no
frame draws or implies one. No claim names the VA building, so no real facility is drawn. The article's date is in its page
metadata and not its text, so no surface prints a date.

Docket item tx-2026-0249, Bexar County, health-and-education.
