# Director brief, November 19th, 2026, carousel no. 82 (BESPOKE + LOOK V2)

## The story (docket tx-2026-0252, claims in out/2026-11-19/claims.json, c1 to c24, the ONLY facts you may use)
UT Arlington is coordinating a $3.1 million USDA funded project (c1) against New World screwworm, a parasite
declared eliminated from the US in 1966 that has reemerged with more than 120 confirmed animal cases as of
November 12th, most of them in Texas (c2). Female flies lay eggs in open wounds and untreated infestations can
be fatal (c3). The center of the project is an AI smart trap meant to tell wild flies from sterile ones and
females from males (c4), keeping wild females because they produce the larvae (c5, c9) and releasing sterile
flies and other insects. Today's traps catch everything and need sorting by hand (c7), and can interfere with the
federal sterile fly program because they can't tell sterile from wild (c8). Each detection could feed alerts and
risk maps to USDA APHIS (c10). The trap is NOT built: a prototype is expected next spring (c6), the project runs
through June 2028 with fieldwork in Texas, other states and Mexico (c12), one of 40 projects in roughly $105
million of USDA funding (c11). Context, already happening: USDA's Kerrville lab uses AI models to pick where, when
and how many sterile flies to release (c16 to c19), models that can't predict every scenario (c20). A dispersal
facility at Moore Air Force Base in Edinburg (c21), $8.5 million, deploys more than 80 million sterile flies a week
over Texas and Mexico per an APHIS page (c22). USDA bred a male only strain for releases (c23). USDA has monitored
more than 100 screwworm specific traps since July 2025 (c24).
