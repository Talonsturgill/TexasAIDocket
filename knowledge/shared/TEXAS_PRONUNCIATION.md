# Texas pronunciation — the narrator has to say these right

Compiled 2026-08-11. This governs the video Dispatch's narrated read and any other spoken
surface. A single mispronounced county name tells a Texas listener the piece was made
somewhere else, and no amount of correct reporting after that gets the credibility back.

## Source, and why it is unusually good

The primary source is the **Texas Almanac Pronunciation Guide**, which is not crowd-sourced. Per
its own front matter it derives from a list compiled in the late 1940s by **George Mitchel
Stokes**, then a Baylor graduate student, who developed pronunciations for 2,300 place names
across the state, later earned his doctorate, and directed Baylor's speech division until 1983.
**2,061 entries were transcribed programmatically** from the guide's proprietary diacritical
font, so entries marked `[TA]` are direct transcriptions and not recollection.

Confidence marks used below:

| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **A** | Two independent sources agree (Texas Almanac and Wikipedia) |
| **B** | One authoritative non-crowd source |
| **C** | Wikipedia only. Usually right on famous names, thin on small towns |
| **D** | **Unverified.** Check with a local before it is spoken |
| **⚠** | Genuinely contested. The disagreement is noted rather than resolved |

## 1. The four modernization rules, which come before everything else

**The source is roughly eighty years old.** Read literally it will make the narrator sound like a
1948 radio announcer, in a specific and mockable way. These four shifts must be applied to every
entry. This is the highest-value section in the document.

| # | Shift | The 1940s source writes | **Synthesize in 2026** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Yod dropping after coronals** (t, d, n, s, l), near universal in modern Texas English | `nyoo BRAHN-f'ls`, `nyoo AY sis`, `styoo di BYOOT` | `noo BRAWN fulz`, `noo AY sis`, `STOO dee BYOOT` |
| 1b | **Yod RETAINED after labials and velars, and word initially.** Do not over-apply rule 1 | `BYOO duh`, `MYOOL shoo`, `yoo VAL di` | Keep exactly as written |
| 2 | **wh becomes w** (the whine-wine merger) | `HWEE ler`, `HWAWRT n`, `HWIT har uhl` | `WEE ler`, `WAWR tn`, `WIT hair ul` |
| 3 | **The pin-pen merger is baked into the source.** Do NOT reproduce it in a broadcast voice | `BIN bruuk` (Benbrook), `PIN wel` (Penwell) | `BEN bruuk`, `PEN wel` |
| 4 | **Intervocalic t flapping is already there.** Keep it, it is correct and it sounds Texan | `buh GOH duh` (Bogata) | Keep the flap |

**The fifth rule saves you on air.** In this respelling system `a + r` is the *air* vowel, not the
*car* vowel. `blar` is BLAIR. `TAR uhnt` is TAIR unt (Tarrant). And critically, **`bar` is BAIR**,
which is Bexar. `ah + r` is the *car* vowel: `BAHR ker` is BAR ker.

## 2. The pattern to internalize before any individual word

Texas place names come from Spanish, German, Czech, Polish, French and a dozen Indigenous
languages, **and the local pronunciation is very often not the origin language's**. The names were
absorbed into English by nineteenth century settlers reading them off maps and deeds, and the
anglicized form is now the correct form.

**Saying the "authentic" origin-language version is not sophistication. It is the loudest outsider
signal available.**

The cleanest demonstration lives inside one word:

| Word | As a town | As a landform | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Llano** | Llano, Texas is **LAN oh** (English ll) | Llano Estacado is **YAH noh es tuh KAH doh** (Spanish ll) | The town anglicized. The region name stayed a Spanish phrase |

| Origin | Written | Origin-language reading | **Actual Texas reading** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spanish | Amarillo | ah mah REE yoh | **am uh RIL oh** |
| Spanish | Refugio | reh FOO hee oh | **ruh FYOO ree oh** |
| Spanish | Palacios | pah LAH see ohs | **puh LASH us** |
| Spanish | Manchaca | mahn CHAH kah | **MAN shak** |
| Spanish | Del Valle | del VAH yay | **del VAL ee** |
| Spanish | Salado | sah LAH doh | **suh LAY doh** |
| Spanish | Lamesa | lah MAY sah | **luh MEE suh** |
| Spanish | San Marcos | sahn MAR kohs | **san MAR kus** |
| Spanish | Rio Grande | REE oh GRAHN day | **REE oh GRAND** |
| German | Boerne | BUR nuh | **BURN ee** |
| German | Gruene | GRÜ nuh | **GREEN** |
| German | Pflugerville | PFLOO ger vil | **FLOO ger vil**, P silent |
| Polish | Kosciusko | kosh CHOOSH koh | **kuh SHOOS koh** ⚠ |
| French | D'Hanis | dah NEE | **duh HAN iss** ⚠ |
| Anglo | Humble | HUM bul | **UM bul**, H silent |
| Anglo | Bowie | BOH ee | **BOO ee** |
| Anglo | Palestine | PAL uh styne | **PAL uh steen** |

**The one systematic exception:** names that are still live Spanish phrases rather than settled
town names keep more Spanish. Llano Estacado, Rio Grande as the river word, Tejano, Tejas, Ysleta
del Sur. And in South Texas and El Paso, bilingual speakers routinely code switch, saying Refugio
the anglicized way in English and the Spanish way in Spanish within one conversation, **with
neither being wrong**.

**Guadalupe is referent-dependent and worth knowing cold.** The river, the mountains, the county
and Austin's Guadalupe Street (the Drag) are all **GWAD uh loop**. The religious referent, Our
Lady of Guadalupe, keeps Spanish **gwah dah LOO peh**. Same spelling, two correct readings,
chosen by what is being named. `[B ⚠]`

### Houston, fully disambiguated

**Every Texas Houston is HYOO stun.** The city, the county, Sam Houston, the university, and
Houston Street in both downtown Houston and downtown San Antonio.

The only exception anywhere is **Houston Street in Manhattan, which is HOW stun** `[C]`, and which
is where SoHo gets its name. If the publication ever has cause to mention it, the contrast is
worth one sentence, because Texans enjoy it.

`[TA]` records an older Texas form, `YOOS tun` with no H glide. **It is archaic. Do not use it.**

### Brands a Texas narrator says weekly

**H-E-B** is spelled out, **AITCH ee bee**, never "heb" `[D]`. **Buc-ee's** is **BUCK eez**.
**Whataburger** compresses colloquially to **WATT er burger** `[D ⚠]`. **Dr Pepper** takes **no
period after Dr**, which is a real style trap. **Reveille**, the A&M mascot, is **REV uh lee** in
the US military style, not the French.

## 4. Highest-risk rows, verify before first broadcast

Bexar (the two-syllable variant), Balcones, Terlingua (stress is contested: `[TA]` marks TER,
Wikipedia marks LING), Kosciusko, Cibolo (`[TA]` stresses first, SIB uh loh is widely heard in San
Antonio today), Copperas Cove (two syllables or three), D'Hanis (HAN or HEN), Zavalla in Angelina
County versus Zavala County in South Texas (different places, different vowels), Yturria,
Kokernot, Coahuiltecan, Praha.

## 5. What this owes the build

**A pronunciation lexicon belongs in the repo as DATA, not as prose.** The narrator pipeline
should consult a machine-readable map rather than have a model recall this file, for the same
reason numerals are computed rather than generated: a recalled pronunciation is a guess with good
posture.

Planned for Wave 7, in `TexasAIDispatch`:

- `assets/lexicon/tx_pronunciation.json`, one record per name carrying the written form, the
  respelling, IPA where known, the confidence mark, the source, and whether the four
  modernization rules have already been applied to the stored value.
- A preflight check that **every proper noun in a VO script resolves against the lexicon**, and
  fails the render when one does not. An unknown place name is not a warning, it is a stop:
  guessing is exactly what this document exists to prevent.
- The 2,061 extracted entries are the seed. This file holds the roughly 230 that matter most.

Until that exists, any spoken proper noun not in this file is **unverified**, and the honest move
is to rewrite the line to avoid it rather than to guess at it.
