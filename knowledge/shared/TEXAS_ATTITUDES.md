# What Texans actually think

Compiled 2026-08-11 from primary crosstabs, not summaries. This is the evidence base for the
editorial voice. Where it contradicts an assumption in another document in this repo, **this
document wins**, because it is sourced to instruments with published methodology.

## The two sources, and when to use which

**2026 Texas Lyceum Poll.** Fielded April 3rd to 13th, 2026. 1,000 live-interviewer telephone
interviews of Texas **adults** plus 200 online with confirmed unregistered adults, n=1,200,
900 registered voters. Spanish-language instrument with bilingual interviewers. Weighted to Texas
Demographic Center 2024 projections. MOE 2.83. Twentieth annual edition.

**UT / Texas Politics Project, June 2026.** Fielded June 5th to 12th, 2026. YouGov panel, 1,251
interviewed, matched to 1,200 **registered voters**. MOE 2.83, 3.47 adjusted. 74th in a series
running since 2008.

**Use Lyceum for what Texans think. Use TPP for what Texas voters think and for trend lines.**
Lyceum samples adults and interviews in Spanish, so it does not undercount young, Latino and
non-citizen-adjacent households the way voter-file polling does.

## 1. The finding that should govern our tone

**The news media is the least-trusted institution in Texas.** TPP December 2025, favorable minus
unfavorable, registered voters:

| Institution | Net |
|---|---|
| Local businesses in your area | **+62** |
| The US military | **+53** |
| The police | +31 |
| Churches and faith organizations | +27 |
| Local government where you live | +16 |
| Universities and colleges | +13 |
| Labor unions | +11 |
| Public schools | +9 |
| The FBI | +7 |
| **Texas state government** | **+6** |
| The criminal justice system | -21 |
| **The federal government** | **-25** |
| **The news media** | **-29** (22 favorable, 51 unfavorable) |

**Press authority is a negative asset here.** "Trust us, we are journalists" actively costs
credibility in Texas. Everything has to come from the document: link the PUCT filing, cite the
queue number, name the vote count.

This is the strongest external justification for the compute-not-generate law in `CLAUDE.md`. In a
state that trusts media less than it trusts the federal government, **a recomputable number is not
a nicety, it is the entire basis on which a reader might believe us.**

Two other things in that table matter. **Local business is the most-liked institution in Texas by a
wide margin**, which tells us the register that works. And state government's net favorability
**fell from +28 to +6 in one year** while local government fell from +35 to +16, so the
anti-institutional mood is neither partisan nor aimed only at Washington.

## 2. Data centers, the best-documented thing on our beat

**TPP June 2026, Q43, construction of a data center in your community:**

**Strongly support 9 · Somewhat support 20 · Somewhat oppose 14 · Strongly oppose 42 · DK 16**

**29 percent support, 56 percent oppose, and strong opposition alone outnumbers total support.**

| Group | Support | Oppose | (Strongly oppose) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rural** | 22 | **62** | (50) |
| **Suburban** | 25 | **60** | (46) |
| Urban | 36 | 45 | |
| **Republicans** | 42 | 44 | (30) |
| Democrats | 15 | 71 | |
| Independents | 17 | 62 | |
| **Republican men** | **52** | 38 | |
| **Republican women** | **31** | **50** | |
| Suburban women | 18 | **65** | |

Three corrections to the obvious framing, each of which national coverage gets wrong:

1. **This is not a left-coded issue.** Republicans split 42 to 44. It is a homeowner issue and a
   women's issue.
2. **The gender split inside the Republican coalition is the single most consequential number
   here.** Republican men support 52 to 38. Republican women oppose 50 to 31. That is a 21-point
   swing inside one party.
3. **Rural Texans are the most opposed AND the best informed.** 62 percent oppose, and 49 percent
   have heard "a lot" about data centers against 39 percent statewide. **Writing this as "rural
   Texas welcomes investment, cities object" is exactly backwards.**

**AI generally**, TPP June 2026: economy **29 positive / 49 negative**. By age, 18 to 29 splits
**50/38 positive** while 65+ splits 18/58. Concern about AI's impact is now **50 percent "very
concerned", fifth of thirteen economic worries**, above job loss and the national debt, and it
jumped 7 points in two months (43 in April to 50 in June).

On personal impacts Texans are net positive on leisure time and physical health, and net negative
on **career prospects (20/30)** and **mental health (19/26)**. **The shape: Texans expect AI to
hurt the economy and their careers but not their daily lives.** The anxiety is macro and abstract.
The data center anger is local and concrete. **They are different emotions and should not be
written as one.**

**Gap in the public record:** no Texas polling exists on AI in schools, AI in healthcare, or AI
surveillance. TPP's AI tag holds 18 items, all economy and data-center focused.

## 3. Energy, where the consensus is much wider than expected

**Lyceum 2026, Q27, importance to the Texas economy:**

**Oil and gas: 72 percent very important, 22 somewhat. 94 percent important. Only 5 percent say
not important.** Democrats 70 very, Republicans 76. Liberals 68, conservatives 75. Hispanic 69,
Black 71. Urban 75, rural 72.

**There is effectively no partisan, ethnic or geographic disagreement in Texas about whether oil
and gas matters.** An Austin liberal and a Midland conservative answer within the margin of error.
It is the closest thing to consensus in Texas public opinion.

**Alternative energy: 41 percent very important, 41 somewhat. 82 percent important.** Among
conservatives **only 25 percent say it is unimportant**. Black Texans are the most supportive at
51 percent very important.

**Renewables are not a culture-war object in Texas.** They are a line item that already won on
price. What they lack is primacy: only 3 percent name alternative energy as the *most* important
sector against 35 percent for oil and gas.

**The physical reality has outrun the political framing.** Per ERCOT CEO Pablo Vegas's July 2026
testimony: record peak **91,308 MW on July 22nd, 2026**, no conservation calls needed, a **20,000
MW buffer** at the record. **Solar at times exceeded 45 percent of all generation.** Battery
discharge record 11,980 MW. Solar capacity more than doubled 2023 to 2025 and storage more than
tripled. **Of roughly 460,000 MW in the queue, about 70 percent is solar and batteries.** The
period of greatest stress has moved from mid-afternoon to late evening.

**Nothing in the 2026 data supports "frozen wind turbines" as a live public belief.** Solar and
storage are the visible reason summer peaks now pass without conservation calls, and Texans have
noticed.

**Uri and the grid.** Abbott's grid approval bottomed at -21 net in June 2022, recovered to +4 by
February 2024, and has slipped back to **-4 in June 2026** — not because of blackouts, but because
the conversation moved to bills and data centers. In April 2025, **82 percent called improving
grid reliability extremely or very important**, the highest-rated item tested, and **54 percent
said the state spends too little on electric infrastructure** against 3 percent too much.

**Electricity bills are a felt grievance that is not yet a named priority.** 37 percent rate their
electricity cost and reliability poor or terrible, concentrated among women (43), renters (42),
no-college (41), rural (41) and Democrats (44). But in the open-ended most-important-problem
question, **energy and the grid draws 3 percent and utility rates draws 1 percent.**

**That gap is the opportunity and the discipline.** Do not tell Texans the grid is their top
issue, because it is not. Tell them why their bill is doing what it is doing.

## 4. Water, the sleeping issue

**Lyceum 2026, Q22: a big problem 16 · somewhat 34 · not much of a problem 43 · DK 7.**

**A plurality of Texans say water is not much of a problem where they live.** Rural is highest at
19 percent "big problem", still under one in five. Awareness of the Corpus Christi water crisis
ranked **dead last of twelve news items**, 15 percent "a lot", 30 percent "nothing at all".

**Implication.** We cannot assume shared alarm. Linking data center water consumption to local
supply is **genuinely new information** for most readers rather than a rehearsed argument. The San
Marcos fight turned on a development calculated to draw more than 25 million gallons a year from
local aquifers, and that arithmetic is the kind of thing this publication exists to compute.

## 5. Property tax, the bridge to our beat

**68 percent say property taxes have a major impact on their finances**, up from 58 percent in
2023. Republicans 76, **Democrats 62**. A 14-point partisan gap in a state where nothing else is
that close. Asked which tax hits hardest: property 52, sales 23.

**And only 16 percent have heard "a lot" about efforts to reduce property taxes**, the second
lowest awareness item tested, while **59 percent of homeowners pay "a lot" of attention to their
property tax bill.**

**An enormous, intensely felt, bipartisan grievance with almost nothing feeding it.** Property tax
is also the mechanism by which a data center pays for itself or does not, through school district
abatements and county tax breaks. **That is the strongest available bridge between this beat and
what Texans already care about most:** data centers, power demand, transmission cost, your bill,
and the abatements that decide whether the facility pays its own way.

## 6. The Texas Miracle is in trouble, and Texans said so first

**Lyceum 2026, Q10: Texas economy better than the rest of the country 26 · worse 37 · same 32.**

Between 2009 and 2018 a majority said better every single year, with no more than 15 percent
saying worse. The Lyceum calls the reversal **"a crisis of faith in the Texas economy"** and its
research director is on record that "the era of the Texas Miracle may be at stake."

**Only 27 percent expect their children to be better off, tied for the lowest in roughly 20 years
of Lyceum polling.**

The split is not primarily partisan. **Women say worse 41 to 17**, a 41-point gender gap and the
largest single-item gap in the entire dataset. Non-graduates 40 to 19. Under $75k, 40 to 23.

**Related and counterintuitive:** the Lyceum's 15-year report documents "white despair" — among
white Texans a majority has **never** said their children will be better off, while Hispanic and
Black Texans said better off in 10 of 14 surveys. In 2026 **Black Texans are the only group with a
positive balance**, 31 better to 30 worse.

## 7. Hispanic Texans, where national coverage is most wrong

**Immigration helps or hurts** (Lyceum 2026, Q32): Hispanic Texans **31 helps / 33 hurts**. White
Texans 29 / 34. **Statistically identical.** The partisan gap (Democrats 48 helps, Republicans 20)
is enormous; the ethnic gap is roughly zero.

On the most important problem facing Texas, **Hispanic Texans name illegal immigration at 14
percent, essentially the same as white Texans at 15.**

The 2024 result: Trump won Texas by **13.7 points**, carried **every border county except El Paso
and Presidio**, and flipped ten counties Biden won including Cameron, Hidalgo, Webb, Starr and
Maverick. **Maverick County, 95 percent Hispanic, swung about 28 points right, Trump's largest
county gain anywhere in the United States. Starr County, the most Hispanic county in the country,
went Republican for the first time since 1892.** Tarrant County (Fort Worth) and Williamson County
also flipped right.

**The honest synthesis:** these are working-class voters in an oil, gas, border-enforcement and
trucking economy, in counties where Border Patrol and CBP are among the largest employers, who
have gotten poorer in real terms and were never socially liberal. Coverage treated them as a
Democratic bloc defined by ethnicity. They were a working-class bloc defined by economics.

**Do not write the Valley as a Democratic stronghold. Do not write it as a Republican one either.
It moved once and can move again.** The June 2026 Senate race polls at 43 to 42.

## 8. Where Texans get news, and what that means for our surfaces

TPP August 2024, used as a news source in the last week: social media 47, cable news 45, **local
television news 44**, broadcast 42, YouTube 32, local newspapers 27, national newspapers 26.

Platforms used weekly: **Facebook 75, YouTube 70**, Instagram 49, **X 38**, TikTok 32, LinkedIn
21, Reddit 18.

**We are building for Facebook and YouTube, not X.** Local TV news is the most-consumed
institutional source, and its visual grammar — plain, a person talking, a chart that reads in
three seconds — is the register this audience is fluent in.

**A visible AI policy is table stakes.** The Texas Tribune labels every automated-audio story with
a note that humans wrote and edited it, and links its AI policy. For a publication *about* AI, in
the most media-skeptical state in the country, this is not optional. It belongs on the site from
launch.

## 9. Two literacy findings that set our explanatory floor

**Only 45 percent of Texas voters know the Railroad Commission regulates oil and gas.** 23 percent
said the Public Utility Commission, 20 percent did not know. In the state that invented the modern
oil industry.

**12 percent of Texans name farming and agriculture as the most important sector of the Texas
economy**, roughly triple its share of GDP, and the belief is held statewide (suburban 11 percent,
Democrats 12) rather than only in rural areas.

**Never assume agency literacy.** Every ERCOT, PUCT and RRC reference gets a gloss, every time,
and the gloss must not condescend. "The Railroad Commission, which regulates oil and gas and no
railroads" does both jobs in eleven words.

## 10. What resonates, and what gets dismissed

**Works:**
- **Bills, reliability, and who pays.** Abbott has already adopted this frame himself, directing
  that data centers fully fund the infrastructure needed to serve them.
- **Specificity about money and mechanism.** "This facility got a $X abatement from this school
  district and uses Y gallons a day from this aquifer", not "big tech is taking over".
- **Local before state before federal**, matching the trust ordering exactly.
- **Both-and on trade-offs.** Texans comfortably hold that oil and gas is 94 percent important AND
  alternative energy is 82 percent important, and that they want both a pathway to citizenship and
  increased border security. **Arguments that force a choice get rejected.**

**Fails:**
- **Climate-first framing.** Environment, climate and water combined draw 4 percent as a most
  important problem. This does not mean Texans deny climate change. It means climate is not the
  currency. The same facts priced in reliability and dollars work fine.
- **Anti-oil-and-gas framing.** 94 percent call it important, including 95 percent of Democrats.
- **"Texas is finally turning blue."** Fifteen years of this claim, and a 13.7-point margin.
- **Explaining Texas to Texans.** Any sentence starting "Texans may not realize that".
- **Renewables as a liberal cause.** The biggest wind buildout in the country is in the reddest
  region of the state and landowners understand royalties.

**The posture, which is the transferable thing:** treat Texas as a place with internal arguments
rather than a character with traits. Flattery and condescension are the same error, because both
treat Texas as a single actor. **Assume the reader knows more about their county than we do, and
less about the interconnection queue than we do. That asymmetry is the entire value proposition.**

## 11. The 2026 data center fight, as of compile date

- **August 3rd, 2026: Abbott ordered a moratorium** on data center approvals pending a PUCT and
  ERCOT audit of every project in the interconnection queue. Developers must disclose tax breaks,
  power use, water use and cooling, community impact mitigation, and ownership. ERCOT paused its
  batch-zero review.
- **ERCOT is tracking more than 1,800 projects totalling over 474 GW**, more than five times the
  grid's all-time record peak. Roughly 90 percent of new power requests are data centers.
- **At least 335 operating data centers and 248 planned.** Texas is second behind Virginia and
  positioned to take first. **Nearly half the planned facilities are in unincorporated areas**,
  outside city zoning authority.
- **There is no state or federal database of these facilities.**
- **Of 377 companies the PUCT notified for its water and power survey, 28 responded.** A
  Republican House member called participation "pretty pathetic".
- Local revolt versus preemption: Hood County rejected a moratorium twice, Hill County passed one
  and **rescinded it after a developer sued for $100 million**, and **San Marcos became the first
  Texas city to ban data centers, 4 to 3 on June 16th**, using home-rule zoning. A Republican
  senator has said he will challenge it.
- **El Paso is not on ERCOT**, so it sits outside the moratorium's reach while hosting several
  large data centers. This is a genuinely underreported angle and it is ours to take.

**This is a Republican-on-Republican fight.** Abbott and the Speaker against parts of the industry,
a Republican senator against local governments, rural and suburban Republican voters against all of
them. **Writing it as "red state welcomes AI, environmentalists object" would be flatly wrong.**

## 12. Claims we must NOT publish without further work

- **Any "Texas is a net federal taker" figure.** Not verified, the framing is contested, and the
  numbers move year to year. Requires the Rockefeller Institute Balance of Payments report or
  Comptroller data directly.
- **Any quantified Texas-identity or state-pride statistic.** Neither Lyceum nor TPP has ever asked
  it, in 20 and 18 years respectively. The claim is probably directionally true and **there is no
  instrument behind it**.
- **Exact current race and ethnicity shares.** The best verified figures are ACS 2021 (Hispanic
  40.2, non-Hispanic white 39.3, Black 11.6, Asian 5.1). Pull Texas Demographic Center Vintage
  2024 before publishing, and note their May 11th, 2026 correction.
- **The urbanization percentage.** Commonly cited as 83 to 84 percent. Not verified.
- **Asian American Texan crosstabs.** Lyceum reports this group only in an "Other" category with
  DK rates of 12 to 29 percent, a sign of small cells. **Treat Lyceum "Other" as unusable.**
- **Border-county attitudinal crosstabs.** Neither major poll publishes a border cut. The claim
  that border opinion differs sharply from statewide opinion was truer a decade ago; **do not
  import the pre-2020 version.**
