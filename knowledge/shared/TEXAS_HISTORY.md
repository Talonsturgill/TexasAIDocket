# Texas history — what is settled, what is contested, and what never to state as fact

Compiled 2026-08-11, primarily from the Texas State Historical Association's Handbook of Texas,
the National Park Service, the Texas State Library and Archives Commission, and Refusing to
Forget. Marks: **[V]** verified this session, **[H]** standard in the literature but not
re-verified, **[C]** genuinely contested, **[?]** unverified lead.

The research pass ran out of search budget before reaching 2023 to 2026 material. The weakest
sections are recent legislative developments and current ERCOT figures. Those are marked.

---

## 1. The single most useful frame

**The popular version of Texas history is substantially a product of the 1930s, not the 1830s.**

The 1936 Texas Centennial was a state-funded, Depression-era identity project. It produced the
iconography most people mean when they picture Texas history: the Alamo Cenotaph (built 1936,
dedicated November 11th, 1940 **[V]**), the San Jacinto Monument, Fair Park's Art Deco complex.
The year before, Walter Prescott Webb published *The Texas Rangers*, which became canonical and
which modern scholars regard as substantially mythmaking **[H]**.

So be careful writing "Texas has always seen itself as." A great deal of that self-conception
has a birth certificate and it reads 1935 to 1940.

**For artwork this matters directly:** the visual vocabulary of "Texas" — Art Deco heroic
figures, the stylized star, Depression-modern monumentality — is a specific, datable design
language. It is not 19th century. Using it knowingly is good. Using it as though it were
period-accurate to 1836 is an error a Texan will notice.

## 2. The structural fact that explains our entire beat

**Texas kept its public lands at annexation, because it kept its debts.**

No other state entered the Union owning its unappropriated land. The consequences are still
running:

- Texas has almost no federal land, roughly 1.8% **[H]**, among the lowest in the West. **There
  is no BLM to fight in Texas.** The land is private, or it belongs to the schools and the
  universities.
- The **General Land Office**, the **Permanent School Fund** and **University Lands** exist
  because of it. University Lands holds about 2.1 million acres in West Texas whose oil, gas and
  now wind and solar royalties fund UT and Texas A&M **[H]**. **When you write about West Texas
  energy land, some of it is literally the university endowment.**
- The **XIT Ranch** is the cleanest illustration: three million Panhandle acres granted to a
  Chicago syndicate in exchange for building the state capitol. **Texas paid for its capitol
  building in land**, because land is what it had instead of federal money.

The **Compromise of 1850** fixed the modern borders: Texas gave up everything north and west of
a defined line, now in five other states, for **$10 million**, much of it earmarked to pay
Republic debt. Republic bondholders lobbied hard for passage **[V]**. The shape of Texas was
substantially determined by creditors.

## 3. The energy through-line, which is the best history we have

This is the thread most worth knowing, because it explains why Texas energy regulation looks the
way it does.

**Spindletop, January 10th, 1901.** Anthony Lucas drilled it; **Pattillo Higgins** was the man
who believed oil was under the hill and had been forced out by 1895 **[V]**. The gusher flowed an
estimated 100,000 barrels a day, more than every other producing US well combined. Texaco, Gulf,
Sun, Magnolia and Humble (later Exxon) grew out of it **[V]**.

Then it collapsed: 100,000 barrels a day to 10,000 in three years, because everyone drilled as
fast as possible under the **rule of capture**, the doctrine that oil belongs to whoever gets it
out first, which makes conservation individually irrational. **The next thirty years of Texas oil
history is about fixing that.**

**East Texas, October 3rd, 1930.** "Dad" Joiner's Daisy Bradford No. 3 opened the largest
reservoir in the contiguous United States: 140,000 acres, ultimately 5.2 billion barrels from
about 30,340 wells **[V]**. Price went from 99 cents a barrel to thirteen cents by mid-1931. In
August 1931 the Governor declared martial law and sent the National Guard to shut in all 1,644
wells **[V]**.

**The Railroad Commission became a price cartel.** It set allowables per well, and "by managing
the flow of oil from America's most important oil fields, the Railroad Commission effectively
determined world prices for the next four decades" **[V]**. **OPEC copied the system** — the
documented version is that OPEC's founders studied Texas prorationing and hired the Commission's
chief engineer to explain it **[V]**.

**The handoff is the best fact in Texas energy history:** in **March 1972 the Commission set
allowables at 100% for the first time**, meaning Texas had no spare capacity left to swing prices
with. **Within about eighteen months, OPEC ran the 1973 embargo.** Global pricing power moved
from Austin to Vienna in roughly a year and a half. **[H — verify the exact date and figure.]**

**The point for our readers:** Texas has never been a free market in energy in the way the
rhetoric suggests. It has been a **managed** one, run by an elected commission, for ninety years.
The Commission still regulates oil and gas, still has three elected commissioners, and has had no
railroad jurisdiction since 2005 **[H]**.

## 4. Why Texas is chasing data centers — the 1986 answer

Oil went from about $35 a barrel in 1981 to **under $10 in 1986** **[H]**. **Texas accounted for
40% of all thrift failures in 1988** **[V]**. Every one of the ten largest Texas bank holding
companies failed or was acquired **[H]** — Texas permanently lost its independent banking
industry. Houston lost roughly 100,000 jobs and downtown towers sat empty for a decade.

**The lesson Texas elites drew was diversification**, and it is the direct ancestor of the tech
recruitment of the 1990s onward, the medical center buildout, the aggressive incentive programs,
and a good deal of the current enthusiasm for AI infrastructure.

**If we write one piece connecting history to the buildout, this is it:** Texas is not chasing
data centers only for cheap land and power. It is chasing them because of 1986.

## 5. Other load-bearing facts

**The name.** From the Caddo *tejas*, meaning friends, and applied to groups **allied against the
Apache** — recorded by a Spanish friar who misunderstood it as the name of a kingdom **[V]**. The
state motto is "Friendship." That chain is the colonial encounter in miniature.

**The Comanche were late arrivals**, a branch of the Northern Shoshone who moved onto the
southern plains in the **late 17th century** after acquiring horses **[V]** — a millennium after
the Caddo, and roughly contemporaneous with the first Spanish missions. Comanche power was built
on horses in the 1690s and destroyed by **killing horses at Palo Duro Canyon in 1874** **[V]**. A
clean 180-year arc, and the single best thing to illustrate.

**The Texas that settlers encountered was already post-apocalyptic.** Epidemic disease ran ahead
of European settlement; much of the die-off happened before most Europeans arrived **[V]**.

**The three federally recognized tribes** are each here under duress from elsewhere: **Ysleta del
Sur Pueblo** (El Paso, refugees of the 1680 Pueblo Revolt, and **the oldest continuously occupied
community in Texas** — older than San Antonio by about 40 years), **Alabama-Coushatta** (Polk
County, from Alabama and Georgia, given land by the state in 1854 largely through Sam Houston),
and the **Kickapoo Traditional Tribe** (Eagle Pass, from the Great Lakes, binational) **[V]**.

**Juneteenth: read the whole order, not the first sentence.** General Order No. 3, Galveston,
June 19th, 1865, proclaims "an absolute equality of personal rights and rights of property" and
then, in the same breath, instructs freed people to stay put, work for wages, and not gather
**[V]**. Emancipation issued as a labor directive. That tension is the actual document and a far
better story than the sentence usually quoted. Freed people later **bought land** for
celebrations because they were barred from public parks, creating the Emancipation Parks **[V]**.

**The cowboy.** The trail-drive era ran roughly **1867 to 1884** — about eighteen years **[V]**.
The occupation is Mexican in origin: vaquero, la reata, chaparreras, rancho, remuda, mesteño,
rodeo, estampida **[V]**. Historians commonly estimate a quarter to a third of trail cowboys were
Black or Mexican, though the empirical base is thinner than the citation frequency suggests
**[C]**. A cowboy was a low-wage seasonal agricultural worker who sometimes went on strike
(the Texas Panhandle cowboy strike of 1883) **[H]**.

**The fence-cutting wars of 1883** are the best historical rhyme for modern transmission and wind
lease fights: a drought, private fences cutting off water, roughly $20 million in fence damage,
and a state response that made cutting a felony **and required ranchers fencing public roads to
install a gate every three miles** **[V]**. The answer to a property war was a compromise about
access. That pattern recurs.

**Galveston, September 8th, 1900** — still the deadliest natural disaster in US history, 6,000 to
12,000 dead **[V]**. The rebuild raised the city by pumping sand underneath it, **physically
jacking up 2,146 buildings** **[V]**. Galveston never recovered its primacy; Houston dredged the
Ship Channel and Spindletop came four months later. **The modern Texas urban hierarchy was set by
a hurricane and a salt dome within four months of each other.**

**Two landmark civil rights cases came out of Texas.** *Smith v. Allwright* (1944) ended the
white primary after Black Texans and the NAACP took the question to the Supreme Court four times
over seventeen years, losing the third time. Registered Black voters in Texas rose from 30,000 in
1940 to 100,000 by 1947 **[V]**. *Sweatt v. Painter* (1950) — Texas built an entire law school
overnight rather than admit one man, which is why Texas Southern University exists **[V]**.

**The realignment took forty years, not one election** (Tower 1961 → the House in 2002), and it
went top-down **[V]**. The right framing: **Texas was not a liberal state that turned
conservative. It was a conservative state that was Democratic and became a conservative state
that is Republican.**

**WWII is the hinge.** Texas entered it rural, agricultural and poor, and left it urban,
industrial and federally subsidized. Manufacturing output went from about $453 million in 1939 to
$1.9 billion in 1944, and about 500,000 people moved to Texas cities for defense work **[V]**.
The Gulf Coast petrochemical complex was substantially built with federal war money.

**Texas Instruments was an oilfield seismic company.** Founded as Geophysical Service in 1930,
renamed TI in 1951; **Jack Kilby demonstrated the first working integrated circuit on September
12th, 1958** **[V]**. Finding oil required signal processing, and signal processing required
electronics. **The line from Spindletop to the integrated circuit to the AI buildout runs through
the same institutions and the same city.**

## 6. The Texas Rangers and the 1910s — get this right

TSHA states flatly that between 1914 and 1919, Rangers and appointed special Rangers "killed
approximately 5,000 Hispanics" **[V]**. **Refusing to Forget**, the historians' project, gives
the range as **"from several hundred to five thousand"** and says the exact toll "remains
unknowable due to incomplete records and the clandestine nature of many killings" **[V]**.

**TSHA prints the top of a range as a point estimate. Do not do that.** Write: *scholars estimate
that state and vigilante violence killed somewhere between several hundred and five thousand
ethnic Mexicans in Texas in the 1910s, and the true figure is unrecoverable because the killings
were deliberately undocumented.* More honest and more damning.

The **Porvenir Massacre**, January 28th, 1918: Rangers, US cavalry and local ranchers executed 15
unarmed Mexican men and boys **[V]**. **José Tomás Canales**, the sole Mexican-American state
legislator, filed nineteen charges in 1919; the hearing transcripts were so damaging the House
refused to print them **[V]**. Cite **Monica Muñoz Martinez, *The Injustice Never Leaves You***
(Harvard, 2018), not the Handbook, on Canales.

**Practical:** the Rangers are still an operating division of DPS and a beloved institution. Say
"the Rangers of the 1910s," never "the Rangers." Tense and scope are the whole difference between
accurate history and an attack on a current agency.

## 7. The slavery question, framed so it survives contact with Texas

Documented and not in dispute **[V]**: enslaved people in Texas went from 443 in Austin's colony
in 1825 (about a quarter of its population) to 182,566 by 1860 (30.2% of the state). Mexico's
1827 state constitution prohibited slavery and colonists evaded it via lifetime hereditary
indentures — slavery with a paperwork costume. Guerrero's abolition decree of September 15th,
1829 was withheld from publication in Texas and Texas was exempted by December 2nd **[V]**.

**The contested part [C]** is how much slavery *caused* the Revolution. Three positions exist:
the traditional federalism account, the strong revisionist account (*Forget the Alamo*, 2021),
and the mainstream academic position, best stated by **Andrew Torget, *Seeds of Empire*** (UNC,
2015): "a complex tangle of cotton, slavery, and Mexican federalism, rather than any single
factor" **[V]**.

**Use this formulation:**
> Historians agree slavery was a substantial cause of the Texas Revolution and disagree about how
> substantial. The strongest recent synthesis treats cotton, slavery and Mexican federalism as
> inseparable rather than ranking them.

That is defensible in any room in Texas. "The Texas Revolution was fought over slavery," stated
flatly, is not, and it will cost readers who would otherwise be persuadable because they will
correctly identify it as an overstatement of the consensus.

**The 1861 Declaration of Causes forecloses the states-rights framing entirely**, and it is a
primary document we can quote: Texas was admitted "holding, maintaining and protecting the
institution known as negro slavery," and the document condemns "the debasing doctrine of the
equality of all men, irrespective of race or color" **[V]**.

**The uncontested facts are damning enough that we never need to overreach on the contested ones.**

## 8. Never state as fact

- **The line in the sand.** First appears in print in 1873, thirty-seven years later, via William
  Zuber, who admitted supplying some of the language himself. No contemporary source **[C]**.
- **A messenger murdered carrying news of emancipation to Texas.** Legend, no documentary basis.
- **Granger reading General Order No. 3 from the Ashton Villa balcony.** There is "no historical
  evidence" for it; copies were posted in public places **[V]**. It is the standard image in
  reenactments and it did not happen.
- **Sheridan's alleged speech to the Texas legislature** urging medals for buffalo hunters.
  Disputed authenticity. We do not need it: the effect is documented even where intent is
  contested.
- **That Texas has a right to secede.** *Texas v. White* (1869) settled it, which is a nice
  irony — the case establishing that no state may leave was about Texas **[V]**.
- **That the Chisholm Trail was called that at the time.** The name attached to an 80-mile
  stretch in Indian Territory and the broad usage emerged **after 1911** **[V]**.
- **How Crockett died.** Many historians count him among those taken alive and executed; TSHA
  says it "still provokes debate" **[V]**. Report the dispute, do not adjudicate it.

**The five-states myth is partly true**, which is why it survives. The 1845 joint resolution does
allow up to four additional states "of convenient size" and has never been repealed **[V]**.
Whether it still has force, and whether it waives congressional consent, is disputed, and most
scholars think it does not **[V]**. Say: *it is a real provision, not a legend, and it is also
not a live option.*

## 9. Vocabulary that marks an insider

**Texian** = the Anglo colonists of the 1820s to 1840s. **Tejano** = Mexican Texans, historically
and today. **Texan** = modern, everyone. Using them interchangeably is an outsider tell.

Do not start Texas history at the Alamo. It starts about 13,500 years earlier; Ysleta is 1680;
San Antonio is 1718. The Alamo was a defeat, in a building already 118 years old, that Houston
wanted abandoned, and it was not strategically decisive **[H]**.

**Goliad killed more Texians than the Alamo** and is far less remembered **[V]**.
**San Jacinto lasted eighteen minutes**, with 630 Mexican dead against 9 Texan **[V]** — the
eighteen minutes is the famous fact and the killing ratio inside them is the honest one.

## 10. Live political fights to handle carefully

- **Alamo interpretation.** The new visitor center and museum opens **spring 2028 [V]**, covering
  the Indigenous era, the Spanish mission period, the Mexican Republic, the battle, and the
  commemoration controversies. We will be reporting on this in 2027. Note the politics are
  **not** left versus right: a Republican land commissioner pushed the broader contextualization
  and a Republican lieutenant governor opposed moving the Cenotaph.
- **Curriculum law.** SB 3 (2021) restricts how race and controversial topics are taught; revised
  social studies standards took effect August 1st, 2024 **[V]**. Subsequent sessions added more
  **[?]**.
- **Confederate monuments.** Texas has removed 31, more than any state **[V]**. Confederate
  Heroes Day, January 19th, and its current status **[?]**.
- **Border and immigration.** The most volatile topic in Texas. Operation Lone Star and its
  litigation **[?]**.
- **Water.** Becoming the biggest constraint story in Texas, with deep history: the 1950s drought
  of record is still the planning benchmark for every state water plan.
