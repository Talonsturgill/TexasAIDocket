# Texas oil economics — how the world reaches Midland, and back

Compiled 2026-08-11 from EIA, the Dallas Fed Energy Survey, BLS, FRED, the Texas Comptroller,
the Railroad Commission, Baker Hughes and trade press. Marks: **[V]** verified against a primary
source, **[1S]** single source, **[?]** unverified.

The research pass had no search budget and worked entirely by direct fetch of known primary
sources. That biased it toward government data, which is good, and away from wire services,
which is a real limitation on the geopolitical chronology.

---

## 1. The finding that reframes our whole energy beat

**The binding constraint on Texas AI buildout is not gas. It is turbines.**

In April 2026 the same commodity was worth **−$7.95/MMBtu at Waha**, **+$2.77 at Henry Hub**,
**+$14.80 at TTF in Europe** and **+$16.02 at JKM in Asia** **[1S, corroborated by EIA on the
Henry Hub and international legs]**. A roughly **$24 spread across four points on one planet**.

That spread is not a molecule shortage. It is a **steel** shortage — pipe, liquefaction trains
and ships. Which means:

> The competition between AI compute and LNG export for Texas gas is not a competition for gas.
> It is a competition to be the fastest way to monetize a stranded molecule. And a gas turbine
> next to the wellhead can be built faster than a pipeline plus a liquefaction train plus a
> tanker.

**And then the turbine queue closed.** Q2 2026 global gas turbine orders hit **38 GW, up 71%
year over year, with the US taking 50%**. Combined-cycle lead times have gone from 3.5 years in
2023 to **five years**. Cost is up **49%** and projected **+195% to about $600/kW by 2027**. At
end-2025 the order book stood at **110 GW against 60 to 70 GW of annual manufacturing
capacity** **[1S]**.

**Free fuel is worthless without a machine to burn it in, and the machine now has a five-year
queue at double the price.** Whoever already holds turbine slots holds an appreciating option.
This is the most decision-relevant number in Texas energy right now and it appears in almost no
general coverage.

**The leading indicator to track: Waha basis.** Waha has held above zero for over a month as of
late July 2026 after new pipeline startups. As it normalizes, the flare-gas-compute pitch gets
harder. Operators themselves expect takeaway constraints resolved around **Q1 2027 (25% of Dallas
Fed respondents)**, though **7% said never** **[V]**.

## 2. The price-to-activity link broke, and that is the story

The textbook chain is price up, rigs up, completions up, employment up, tax revenue up. **In 2026
the first three links failed.**

| Link | Prediction | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Price | WTI $58 (Dec 2025) to $102 (May 2026) | **+76%** ✓ |
| Rig count | sharp rise | **588 US rigs, +9% year over year** |
| Permits | surge | **693 in July against 692 in March, flat** |
| Extraction employment | boom | **fell from 64.2k to 61.9k, January to June** |

A Dallas Fed respondent said it plainly: *"Elevated oil prices are not directly translating into
increased production or increased demand for services."*

**Why:** the shock was **distillate-led**, and diesel is a shale producer's largest single field
input. Dallas Fed Q2 2026, verbatim: *"Diesel fuel costs have risen sharply, up approximately 65
percent since January."* **The war raised the price of the oil West Texas sells and the price of
the diesel West Texas burns to produce it.** A refiner's windfall is a frac crew's cost.

## 3. The two employment lines — a trap for careless writers

**BLS series SMU48000001021100001 covers NAICS 211, oil and gas extraction, the operators. It
fell.** TXOGA reports upstream jobs **rose** for four straight months. **Both are true.** TXOGA's
"upstream" includes NAICS 213112, support activities — the service companies.

Extraction payrolls are shrinking structurally through consolidation and automation, while
service payrolls turn with activity at the margin. **Any story citing one without the other is
wrong, and the divergence is itself the story.** Nobody publishes both.

## 4. The two breakevens

From the Dallas Fed Q1 2026 survey **[V]**:

| | All firms | Large (≥10k b/d) | Small |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cover operating expenses, existing wells** | **$43/bbl** | $32 | $46 |
| **Profitably drill a new well** | **$66/bbl** ($67 Permian) | $59 | $68 |

**Between $43 and $67, existing wells keep pumping and no new wells get drilled.** December 2025
WTI averaged **$57.97** — squarely inside that dead zone, which is why the rig count entered 2026
low and stayed there.

The large-firm and small-firm gap is the consolidation mechanism in two numbers: every downturn
mechanically transfers acreage upward. Q2 2026 verbatim: *"The biggest issues are the lack of
acreage and the cost of acquisitions. It's challenging for independents."*

## 5. The shale put has been repriced, and operators said so

Dallas Fed asked what US production growth would result at **$100, $125 and $150** WTI. The
most-selected answer **at all three price levels** was **"more than 0.25 but not more than 0.50
million b/d"** **[V]**.

**Operators say $150 oil buys roughly the same response as $100 oil.** In 2014 a comparable
signal would have been expected to add 1.0 to 1.5 million b/d. The put has not disappeared; it
has been **repriced to a much higher strike and a much smaller quantity**.

Why: capital discipline, tier-1 inventory depletion, service and input inflation, a shrinking
tight workforce, and — the non-obvious one — **gas takeaway. You cannot drill more oil wells than
you can dispose of associated gas.** Negative Waha is a hard stop on oil growth.

**What actually capped the 2026 price was not new drilling.** It was exports of existing
production, refinery throughput, Chinese strategic reserve drawdown, and Asian demand
destruction.

## 6. The trade flows — two structural breaks

US crude exports by destination, thousand b/d **[V, EIA]**:

| Destination | 2023 | 2025 | **May 2026** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Japan** | 44 | 106 | **1,084** |
| Netherlands | 624 | 886 | 1,174 |
| South Korea | 403 | 507 | 829 |
| **China** | **459** | **23** | — |

**Japan went from nothing to number two in ninety days** — a 47-fold increase. Japan imports over
90% of its crude through Hormuz. When the Strait shut, it had one place to go at scale, and it
was Corpus Christi and Houston.

**China went to zero.** From 459 to 23 kb/d, a 95% collapse, and that is the trade war rather
than the crisis. **Any piece claiming Texas crude "feeds China" is three years out of date.**

**This is the two-way causation in one table.** A missile fired in the Persian Gulf in February
raised the price a refiner in Chiba pays, which raised the bid for a barrel loading at Ingleside,
which raised the netback to a working-interest owner in Martin County, which raised the severance
deposit in Austin. No step in that chain is a metaphor.

**April 2026 was the largest month of US petroleum exports in history: 13.6 million b/d, 15%
above the record set the month before**, with crude alone at 5.6 million b/d **[V]**.

## 7. The Brent to WTI spread as a teaching instrument

| Month | Brent | WTI | Spread |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feb 2026 | 70.89 | 64.51 | 6.38 |
| **Mar** | 103.13 | 91.38 | **11.75** |
| **Apr** | 117.29 | 100.32 | **16.97** |
| May | 107.14 | 102.13 | 5.01 |
| **Jun** | 85.40 | 84.81 | **0.59** |
| Aug 11 | 89.16 | 83.44 | 5.72 |

Read as a story: the disruption was in the Eastern Hemisphere, so **Brent repriced instantly and
WTI could not follow, because Cushing is not on the water**. A $17 spread is a $17 signal
screaming *get every barrel to the Gulf Coast*. The system obeyed — exports went from 4.0 to 5.7
million b/d in sixty days — and by June the arbitrage had closed to **59 cents**.

**WTI is a landlocked price and Brent is a seaborne price. The spread is the cost of the pipe and
the ship between them.**

**And since 2023, WTI Midland sits inside the Brent basket.** Brent is no longer a North Sea
benchmark; it is an Atlantic Basin benchmark with a West Texas core. For a Midland reader: **your
barrel now sets the world price directly.**

## 8. The Texas budget, framed correctly

From the Comptroller's 2026-27 Biennial Revenue Estimate **[V]**:

- Oil production tax: **6.7%** of general revenue-related collections
- Natural gas production tax: **3.0%**
- **Combined severance: 9.7%**. Sales tax: **53.4%**

**Three things general coverage gets wrong:**

1. **"Texas depends on oil for about 10% of its budget" badly understates it.** A large share of
   that 53.4% sales tax is oil-derived: Permian equipment, oilfield services, Midland and Odessa
   retail, refinery and petrochemical capex, trucking. The severance line is the *visible*
   dependence. The sales-tax channel is larger and invisible. So are Permanent University Fund
   and Permanent School Fund royalties, which fund education entirely outside this frame.
2. **"The Rainy Day Fund protects Texas" misses the cap.** The Economic Stabilization Fund is
   projected at **$28.48 billion and has hit its constitutional ceiling. No transfer is projected
   for fiscal 2027** **[V]**. Marginal severance revenue above the cap does not recapitalize the
   buffer; it funds current spending. **The buffer is fixed while the exposure grows.**
3. **The industry's own figure is much larger than the tax line** — TXOGA claims $39.6 billion in
   taxes and royalties in FY2025 **[1S, trade association, label it as such]**, which includes
   school district property taxes and university land royalties the 9.7% excludes.

## 9. Who wins and who loses — run this ledger every shock

The 2026 shock, honestly scored:

| Constituency | Effect | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Royalty and mineral owners** | **biggest winners** | revenue share, zero cost exposure, immune to diesel inflation |
| **Refiners** | **enormous** | gasoline cracks +60%, distillate and jet more than doubled, diesel cracks $80 to 85/bbl, above the price of WTI itself |
| **Midstream and export terminals** | large | volume-based, record exports, indifferent to price level |
| Large public E&Ps | good | $59 breakeven against $102 realizations, capped by capital discipline |
| Small independents | mixed | $68 breakeven, priced out of acreage |
| Oilfield service companies | **squeezed** | *"fuel expenses have greatly increased, but equipment pricing is not keeping up"* |
| Gas-weighted Permian producers | **hurt** | paying up to $7.95/MMBtu to dispose of gas during an oil boom |
| Oilfield workers | **ambiguous** | wages rising and crews being poached, **but extraction employment fell**. Higher pay for fewer people |
| Surface owners without minerals | **hurt** | all the traffic and dust, none of the check |
| Texas consumers | **hurt** | gasoline up about $1.16 a gallon since the war started |

**One sentence:** the 2026 shock made refiners, exporters and mineral owners rich; made service
companies and small independents work harder for the same money; cost oilfield workers jobs even
as it raised their wages; and hurt every Texan who buys diesel. **"Good for Texas" is not a
sentence that survives contact with the data.**

## 10. Ownership terms that outsiders never grasp

**Mineral rights and surface rights are routinely severed in Texas, and the mineral estate is
dominant** — it has the legal right to use the surface as reasonably necessary. So the person
whose cattle graze the land often does not own what is beneath it, and gets the truck traffic,
the caliche road and the pad site with no royalty.

**Working interest** pays a share of costs and takes a share of revenue. **Royalty interest**
takes a share of revenue **free of costs**. These are not the same trade and they never move
together. **That distinction is why "the industry" never has one opinion about anything.**

**And `boe` is a lie about value.** Six Mcf equals one barrel of oil equivalent on *energy*
content. At WTI $83 and Henry Hub $2.75 the *economic* ratio is nearer **30 to 1**. A company
reporting boe growth may be growing volume while shrinking revenue.

## 11. Data traps to encode in the checks

- **RRC and EIA production figures do not match.** RRC reported 122.7 million barrels for April
  2026 (about 4.09 million b/d); EIA reports Texas at 5,854 thousand b/d. Different definitions,
  and RRC's first prints are always low and revised upward for months. **Never compare an RRC
  monthly headline to an EIA number without saying so.**
- **BLS NAICS 211 against TXOGA "upstream"** — see section 3.
- **The Permian is not Texas.** **New Mexico's Lea and Eddy counties alone delivered 52% of all
  US oil production growth since 2020**, more than all eight Texas Permian counties combined
  **[V]**. Texas gets the headquarters, the service base, the refineries and the ports. New
  Mexico increasingly gets the barrels.
- **Ownership of Texas infrastructure cuts both ways in a Gulf war.** The largest refinery in the
  United States is Motiva Port Arthur at 730,000 b/d, **owned by the government of Saudi Arabia**.
  Golden Pass LNG is **70% QatarEnergy** **[V]**.

## 12. Recurring explainers nobody currently writes

1. **The four prices of gas** — a weekly Waha, Henry Hub, TTF and JKM board with the spreads
   spelled out. The most vivid economic fact in Texas is published nowhere in one place.
2. **The Brent to WTI spread as the cost of getting Texas to the water**, decomposed into
   tariff, freight and quality.
3. **The two employment lines** — NAICS 211 against 213112, with the divergence explained. This
   settles the "is Midland booming" argument permanently.
4. **"Who got paid?"** — the section 9 ledger, rerun after every meaningful price move.
5. **The turbine queue** — order books, lead times and dollars per kilowatt, framed as *the*
   binding constraint on Texas AI buildout.
6. **"Where did the barrel go?"** — monthly export destinations. Japan 23 to 1,084 and China 459
   to 23 are the two most dramatic energy trade facts of the decade and EIA publishes them
   monthly in a table nobody reads.
7. **The Dallas Fed decoder**, quarterly. The verbatim executive comments are better reporting
   than most published analysis and they are free, primary and quotable.
8. **The Chinese strategic reserve watch.** China cut imports 3.9 million b/d in Q2 2026 while
   cutting refinery runs only 2.2 million b/d, implying a roughly 1.7 million b/d strategic
   drawdown **silently capping the world price**. When it reverses, the market loses a buffer it
   never priced. Almost nobody covers this.

## 13. The best free primary source nobody cites

**The Dallas Fed Energy Survey.** Quarterly, about 130 exploration and service firms in the
Eleventh District, since 2016. Q1 carries the annual breakeven question. It publishes diffusion
indexes, price expectations, and — most valuable — **anonymous verbatim executive comments**.

It is the single best free primary source on how Texas operators actually think, and it is
dramatically under-cited in the general press. Two quarterly releases, in March and June.
